Seven mutations, four hundred mammals

Week 1about 3 hours

A research project of the Chronomic Medicine Group. A few families sleep four to six hours a night, all their lives, and feel fine. Their short sleep has been traced to single letters of their DNA – seven mutations in five genes active in the brain. You will look at the same places in the genomes of over four hundred kinds of mammal, and ask what evolution has done with them.

The question

Each of the seven mutations changes one amino acid of a protein. The papers that found them say that the amino acid is conserved – the same in the species they looked at, a handful of mammals and a fish or a fly – and take that as a sign that it matters. Mammals, though, sleep anything from two or three hours a day (giraffes, horses, cows) to nearly twenty (armadillos, opossums, some bats). If one changed amino acid can shorten sleep in people, has evolution ever made the same change? Do the mammals that sleep little carry different amino acids at these places from the mammals that sleep a lot?

Your project: use the protein sequences of hundreds of mammals to test whether the seven amino acids really are conserved, find where on the mammal family tree they have changed, test whether those changes go with how long species sleep – and look at the same mutations in hundreds of thousands of people.

As far as we know, nobody has done this analysis before. The guide takes you through it in eleven stages, about one a week. Each stage teaches you the tools it needs – you do not need to have programmed before. By the end you will have used a Linux terminal, real bioinformatics programs, R, a 3D viewer of proteins and public databases, and you will have results of your own to write up.

The plan

Stages 1–7 are the core of the project. Stages 8, 9 and 10 each add a separate line of evidence – and stage 10 holds a surprise. Each stage ends with going further: ideas to follow if you have time, or if you want to make the project your own.

How this page works

  • The guide is on the left; the workbench is on the right. The tabs along the top of the guide are the stages; Help is the manual of the tools. Drag the line between the two halves to make either wider.
  • Grey boxes with code have a button: Run in the terminal (terminal commands) or Add to notebook (R code). The buttons save typing, but type the first few commands of each stage yourself – that is how they stick.
  • Checklists like the one below have boxes to tick. What you tick is saved in this browser, and the tab of the stage fills in as you go.
  • Hints and answers hide behind Show a hint. Try first; looking is fine when you are stuck.
  • Search (top of this pane) finds any word in the guide and in the help.
  • New words are explained in the Glossary; the data are described in The project data, and the papers in The reading list.
Your files live in this browser

Everything you make is kept in this browser on this computer – not on a server. Use the same computer and browser each time, and once a week click My files → Download all my files (top right) to keep a copy. In a private window, or after clearing the browser's data, your files are gone.

Working on your own

  • Keep a logbook. Every research project has one. Yours is a text file, ~/project/logbook.md: what you did, what you found, what you did not understand. Bring your questions to your supervisor.
  • One stage a week is about right, with time to read. Some take longer – that is normal.
  • When you are stuck: read the error message (it usually says what is wrong); check the hint; search the guide; ask the AI tab (it needs a key of your own – see The AI assistant – and it can be wrong, so check what it says); write the question in your logbook for your supervisor.
  • Check yourself. The green Check boxes say what you should see. If your numbers differ, go back a step – a small slip early on changes everything after it.

Ground rules

  • All the data here are public. The animal genomes are open to everyone; the human data are counts and summary results of large studies – nobody's own genome, name or medical record. Never put personal or patient data into this workbench or into an AI tool.
  • Cite the data and the programs you use (The project data has the papers). If you use the AI assistant, say so in your report, and how.
  • Your results are yours: the guide shows you how to get them, and the checks tell you when you have; what they mean is for you to think about and discuss.
