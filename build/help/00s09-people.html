Stage 9 · The mutations in people

Weeks 9–10about 5 hours

Each mutation was found in a family or two. Hundreds of thousands of people have now had their exomes sequenced. How many of them carry the mutations – and do the carriers sleep less? Two public resources answer that, and a third predicts how damaging each change should be.

You will learn
  • allele counts and frequencies, and what they say about how rare a mutation is
  • to read the results of an association study: an effect, its confidence interval, its P-value
  • to draw a forest plot in R
  • what a prediction of pathogenicity is – and is not

1. How rare are they?

gnomAD (the Genome Aggregation Database) collects the exomes and genomes of about 807,000 people and reports how often each variant is seen. Open the notebook of this stage:

gn <- read.delim("/data/examples/fnss/population/gnomad_fnss_variants.tsv")
gn[, c("variant_id", "rsid", "ac", "an", "af", "hom")]

ac is the allele count, the number of chromosomes carrying the mutation; an the number of chromosomes looked at (two per person); af = ac / an, the allele frequency; hom the number of people with two copies. A rare mutation is almost always carried on one chromosome only, so the share of people who carry it is about twice the allele frequency:

gn$carriers_one_in <- round(1 / (2 * gn$af))
gn[, c("variant_id", "ac", "af", "carriers_one_in")]
fnss <- gn[gn$variant_id != "NPSR1_N107I", ]
round(1 / sum(2 * fnss$af))
Check

All seven are in gnomAD. The commonest are GRM1 S458A (319 alleles: about 1 in 2,500 people carries it) and ADRB1 A187V (197: 1 in 4,100), then SIK3 N783Y (1 in 8,300) and DEC2 P384R (1 in 13,400); Y206H and Y362H are very rare (4 alleles and 1). Nobody has two copies. Together, about 1 person in 1,150 carries one of the seven. N107I, for comparison, has a frequency of 0.45.

Think

If every carrier slept two hours less than other people, natural short sleepers with one of these seven mutations alone would be about 1 in 1,150 people – some 60,000 in the UK. Is that plausible? What would you expect if the mutations did not always cause short sleep?

By ancestry – the share of chromosomes, per 10,000, in each group:

anc <- read.delim("/data/examples/fnss/population/gnomad_fnss_by_ancestry.tsv")
s458 <- anc[anc$variant_id == "GRM1_S458A", ]
s458$per_10000 <- round(10000 * s458$ac / s458$an, 2)
s458[, c("ancestry_label", "ac", "an", "per_10000")]
Check

S458A is found in people of European ancestry, most often in Finland (5 per 10,000 chromosomes, against 2.3 in other Europeans), a little in admixed Americans, and not at all in the African, East Asian or South Asian groups. Do the same for A187V and N783Y: they too are European.

Other changes at the same codons

same <- read.delim("/data/examples/fnss/population/gnomad_other_changes_same_codon.tsv")
same <- same[same$ac > 0, ]
same$af <- signif(same$ac / same$an, 3)
same[, c("reported_variant", "hgvsp", "ac", "af", "hom")]

A codon can change in more than one way. Look at DEC2 P384: besides P384R, gnomAD has P384L, P384S – and P384Q, glutamine instead of proline, in 990 chromosomes, 1 in 600 people, six of them with two copies.

Think

A different change at the very position of the first short-sleep mutation is carried by one person in 600, some of them homozygous. UniProt notes that in cells P384Q has the opposite effect to P384R (it increases DEC2's repression of CLOCK–BMAL1). What does it tell you that P384 can change to Q in so many healthy people? What would you want to know about them?

2. Do the carriers sleep less?

UK Biobank asked half a million people, among many other things, "About how many hours sleep do you get in every 24 hours? (please include naps)". Genebass (Karczewski et al. 2022) tested the variants in the exomes of 394,841 of them – the people of European ancestry – against thousands of traits, sleep among them. For each mutation: the number of carriers' alleles (ac), the difference in sleep between carriers and everyone else (beta, in hours), its 95% confidence interval and its P-value:

ukb <- read.delim("/data/examples/fnss/population/ukb_exomes_fnss_variants.tsv")
sd <- ukb[ukb$phenotype == "sleep duration", ]
sd[, c("variant_id", "ac", "beta", "ci95_low", "ci95_high", "p", "note")]

A forest plot shows each estimate as a dot and its confidence interval as a line. The red band is roughly how much less the short sleepers of the families slept than their relatives – about one and a half to three hours (He et al. 2009: 6.25 hours against 8.06; the other papers report 1.7 to 3 hours less):

sd <- sd[!is.na(sd$beta) & sd$variant_id != "NPSR1_N107I", ]
n <- nrow(sd)
par(mar = c(4.5, 10, 2, 1))
plot(sd$beta, n:1, xlim = c(-3.5, 1.5), ylim = c(0.5, n + 0.5), pch = 19, yaxt = "n", ylab = "",
     xlab = "Difference in sleep, carriers minus others (hours a day)", main = "UK Biobank exomes")
segments(sd$ci95_low, n:1, sd$ci95_high, n:1)
axis(2, at = n:1, labels = paste0(sd$variant_id, " (", sd$ac, ")"), las = 1, cex.axis = 0.8)
abline(v = 0, lty = 2)
rect(-3, 0, -1.5, n + 1, col = rgb(1, 0, 0, 0.1), border = NA)

segments draws the lines from ci95_low to ci95_high; axis(2, …) writes the names, with the number of carriers' alleles, on the left; rect draws the band, in a see-through red.

Check

Five can be tested (Y362H and R889W are too rare). None goes with shorter sleep: A187V, 166 carriers' alleles, −0.11 hours (95% CI −0.25 to +0.04); S458A, 173, +0.12 (−0.03 to +0.26); N783Y, 101, −0.01 (−0.20 to +0.18); P384R, 29, +0.27 (−0.09 to +0.62). For the first three the interval rules out a difference of an hour or more. Y206H, with 3, says nothing either way (−1.0 to +1.2).

Your turn

The table has five other sleep questions: chronotype, ease of getting up, insomnia, napping and dozing in the day. Look at all the results with a P-value below 0.05: ukb[!is.na(ukb$p) & ukb$p < 0.05, ]. How many tests are there in all for the seven mutations? How many would you expect to have P < 0.05 by chance alone?

Show the answer

One: SIK3 N783Y and insomnia, P = 0.04 – carriers report slightly less insomnia. There are 30 tests of the seven mutations (five mutations × six questions); by chance alone about 30 × 0.05 = 1.5 would have P < 0.05. One is what chance would give.

Think

Before you conclude "the mutations do nothing", list what could explain the difference between the families and UK Biobank. The sleep question is answered in whole hours, by people aged 40 to 70. The families were found because they slept little – perhaps other genes in them help. Mice with the mutations slept less, but a mouse is not a person. And a mutation that shortens sleep in some carriers but not most (low penetrance) would look like this. Weedon et al. (2022) came to the same point with fewer people: compare their conclusion with yours.

3. How damaging should they be?

AlphaMissense (Cheng et al. 2023) predicts, for every possible amino-acid change in every human protein, a score from 0 (harmless) to 1 (damaging), from the protein's structure and from how variable the position is across species. It calls scores above 0.564 likely pathogenic, below 0.34 likely benign, and those between ambiguous. The table has all 19 possible changes at each of the seven positions:

am <- read.delim("/data/examples/fnss/population/alphamissense_fnss_sites.tsv")
am[am$is_fnss_mutation == 1, c("variant", "substitution", "am_score", "am_class")]
table(am$variant, am$am_class)
for (v in unique(am$variant)) {
  a <- am[am$variant == v, ]
  cat(v, ": the short-sleep change ranks", rank(-a$am_score)[a$is_fnss_mutation == 1], "of", nrow(a), "\n")
}

rank(-a$am_score) ranks the 19 changes from the most damaging (1) to the least (19).

v <- unique(am$variant)
par(mar = c(4.5, 10, 2, 1))
stripchart(am_score ~ factor(variant, levels = rev(v)), data = am, pch = 19, col = "grey60", las = 1,
           xlim = c(0, 1), xlab = "AlphaMissense score")
f <- am[am$is_fnss_mutation == 1, ]
points(f$am_score, match(f$variant, rev(v)), pch = 19, col = "red", cex = 1.4)
abline(v = c(0.34, 0.564), lty = 3)
Check

Only Y362H is predicted pathogenic (0.73). P384R (0.41) and Y206H (0.46) are ambiguous; A187V (0.32), N783Y (0.23), R889W (0.14) and S458A (0.11) benign. S458A ranks 18th of the 19 possible changes at its position: one of the most harmless changes there could be. At ADRB1 187, NPSR1 206 and DEC2 362, most other changes are predicted pathogenic.

Think

AlphaMissense learned in part from how variable each position is across species – the question of stage 4. Is it surprising, then, that it calls S458A benign? Can its score be used as independent evidence alongside your stage 4 results?
