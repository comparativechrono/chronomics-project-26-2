Stage 11 · Write it up

Weeks 11–12as long as it takes

You have tested claims that every paper on these mutations made, in more species than anyone before you, and found an animal that carries one of them. Now make it into a report that someone else can read, check and repeat: a story with figures, methods precise enough to follow, and an analysis that runs again from start to finish with one command.

You will learn
  • to turn notebooks and commands into scripts that rebuild every result
  • to record the versions of programs and data, and cite them
  • to plan figures, and write methods, results and discussion
First

Ask your supervisor what the report must look like: its length, its sections, the style of references, the deadline – and whether a poster or a talk goes with it. Then plan backwards from the deadline.

1. The story and the figures

Before you write sentences, decide what the reader should learn, in order, and which figure shows each step. A typical set:

Each figure needs a title, labelled axes with units, a legend, and a caption that says what is shown, how many species or people, and what the reader should notice. Tables are better than figures for a few exact numbers – the amino acids of the seven positions, for example.

2. Methods: what, with what, in what order

Write the methods so that someone else could repeat your analysis. Name every data set (with its source, version or date), every program (with its version) and every step – and every choice you made: the window identity of 0.5, how you combined the two sources, how you matched sleep species to genome species. Get the versions:

kalign --version 2>&1 | head -3
seqtk 2>&1 | grep -i version
Rscript -e 'R.version.string'
Rscript -e 'library(ape); library(phylolm); packageVersion("ape"); packageVersion("phylolm")'

Cite the data and the programs: The project data lists the papers for the data, and Programs and versions for the programs. If you used the AI assistant, say what for.

3. Make it repeatable

Your results came from commands typed in a terminal and cells run in notebooks, over weeks. Could you get exactly the same numbers again? The way to be sure is to put every step in scripts and run them, from nothing, in one go.

  1. For each stage, copy the commands that made your results (from your logbook, or history in the terminal) into a shell script in ~/project/scripts – for example stage4_align.sh. Only the commands that matter, in order, with a comment above each.
  2. Turn each notebook into an R script: in the Files tab, make a new file (the + button) such as ~/project/scripts/stage4_conserved.R, and copy the notebook's code cells into it, in order. (The notebook's Download button also gives you its code as an R script, on your computer.) Every script must read its inputs from files and write its outputs to files – nothing may depend on what happened to be in memory, such as read_fasta defined in another notebook. Save figures with png() and dev.off(). Test each script on its own in the terminal: Rscript scripts/stage4_conserved.R.
  3. Write one script that runs all the others, in order:
#!/bin/bash
# run_all.sh – rebuild every result of the short-sleep project from the data in /data/examples/fnss
set -e                        # stop at the first error
cd ~/project
bash scripts/stage1_mutations.sh
bash scripts/stage2_mammals.sh
bash scripts/stage4_align.sh
Rscript scripts/stage4_conserved.R
Rscript scripts/stage5_tree.R
Rscript scripts/stage6_sleep.R
Rscript scripts/stage7_tests.R
bash scripts/stage8_plddt.sh
Rscript scripts/stage9_people.R
bash scripts/stage10_reads.sh
Rscript scripts/stage10_binomial.R
  1. Test it: move your results away (mv results results_old; mv figures figures_old; mkdir results figures), run bash scripts/run_all.sh, and compare the new results with the old (diff results/species_residues.tsv results_old/species_residues.tsv says nothing if they are identical). Fix whatever breaks – there will be something – until it runs from start to end.
  2. Write ~/project/README.md: what the project is, what is in each folder, and how to run run_all.sh.
Why bother?

Because you will find a mistake in stage 4 the week before the deadline, and with run_all.sh you fix one line and everything after it is right again – the figures too. And because a result that cannot be repeated is not yet a result.

4. Results: numbers with their uncertainty

Report what you found, in the order of the figures, with the numbers that matter and their uncertainty: counts of species with their totals, effects with their 95% confidence intervals and P values, the number of species in every test. Keep interpretation for the discussion. A checklist of the results you have:

  • The seven positions in up to 453 mammals, from two sources that agree in every species they share; the exceptions at the five conserved positions; the two positions that vary.
  • How conserved each position is compared with the rest of its protein; the fault in TOGA's DEC2.
  • When and how often GRM1 458 and SIK3 783 changed.
  • Sleep and the amino acids: the ordinary and the phylogenetic tests, with both sleep compilations.
  • Where the seven sit in the proteins, and how sure AlphaFold is.
  • The mutations in gnomAD and UK Biobank; their AlphaMissense scores; P384Q.
  • The heterozygous porcupine.

5. Discussion

Questions to answer in your own words – there are no answers here, and your supervisor will want to discuss them with you:

  • For each mutation: was the paper's claim of conservation right? What kind of evidence is "conserved in a handful of species", and what does your analysis add?
  • Two of the seven positions vary freely across mammals – one of them (GRM1 S458) among the most variable positions of its protein. What does that say about how likely a change there is to shorten sleep? And a change at a position that never varies?
  • Why did the simple test of GRM1 458 and sleep give a significant result, and the phylogenetic one not? Which do you believe, and why?
  • The carriers of the mutations in UK Biobank do not sleep less. List the possible explanations, and say what evidence would decide between them.
  • What does the porcupine mean? What would you need to know, and how would you find out?
  • What would you do next, with more time, data or money?
