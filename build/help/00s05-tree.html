Stage 5 · Where the changes happened

Weeks 5–6about 5 hours

Two of the seven positions differ between mammals. Did each change once, long ago, and pass to all the descendants – or many times, independently? The family tree of the mammals tells you. In this stage you draw it in R, put the amino acids on it, and work out what the ancestors had.

You will learn
  • trees in R with the package ape: reading, pruning, drawing, measuring
  • what a node of a tree is, and how old it is
  • to infer the states of ancestors with a model of change along the branches (ace)
  • to count the changes – and why that matters for everything that follows

1. The tree of the mammals

mammals.nwk is the dated tree of Upham et al. (2019), cut down to the 485 species of your files. It is in Newick format: brackets for groups, and after each colon the length of a branch in millions of years. Open the notebook of this stage and load ape, the R package for trees (the first time, it is downloaded: it takes a little while):

library(ape)
tree <- read.tree("/data/examples/fnss/tree/mammals.nwk")
tree
max(node.depth.edgelength(tree))

node.depth.edgelength gives, for every tip and node, its distance from the root along the branches: the largest is the age of the root.

Check

485 tips and 484 internal nodes. The root is 179 million years old: the split between the egg-laying platypus and echidna and all other mammals.

A tree of 485 species is too big to read. keep.tip prunes it to the species you name – here twelve – and axisPhylo adds a time scale:

twelve <- c("Homo_sapiens", "Pan_troglodytes", "Mus_musculus", "Rattus_norvegicus", "Canis_lupus", "Felis_catus",
            "Bos_taurus", "Sus_scrofa", "Loxodonta_africana", "Myotis_lucifugus", "Monodelphis_domestica",
            "Ornithorhynchus_anatinus")
small <- keep.tip(tree, twelve)
plot(small)
axisPhylo()

Each node, where branches split, is a common ancestor. The tree is drawn so that the tips line up on the right: today. Read it from the root (left) to the tips.

Your turn

How long ago did humans share an ancestor with mice? With dogs? getMRCA(tree, c("Homo_sapiens", "Mus_musculus")) gives the node of the most recent common ancestor; its age is the root's age minus its distance from the root. Write a little function, age(tree, species).

Show the answer 
age <- function(tr, sp) {
  d <- node.depth.edgelength(tr)
  max(d) - d[getMRCA(tr, sp)]
}
age(tree, c("Homo_sapiens", "Mus_musculus"))
age(tree, c("Homo_sapiens", "Canis_lupus"))

About 75 million years for the mouse, and 82 for the dog – the dog is a slightly more distant relative of ours than the mouse is.

3. What did the ancestors have?

Nobody sequenced the ancestors, but their amino acids can be inferred from their descendants. ace (ancestral character estimation) fits a simple model of evolution: along every branch, S can change to T and T to S, at a rate that is the same both ways (model = "ER", equal rates), and the longer the branch, the more likely a change. It finds the rate that best explains the tips, and gives each node the probability of each amino acid:

fit <- ace(x, t2, type = "discrete", model = "ER")
fit$rates
head(round(fit$lik.anc, 3))

R may print a few warnings (NaNs produced, Inf replaced by maximum positive value): they come from ace's estimate of how uncertain the rate is, which it cannot calculate well here; the rate and the probabilities are fine.

fit$lik.anc has a row for each node – the first is the root – and a column for each amino acid. The great block of T species is the Laurasiatheria: carnivores, hoofed mammals and whales, bats, shrews and hedgehogs. Their common ancestor is the node where the lines to the cow and to the shrew meet. The nodes are numbered after the tips, so its row is its number minus the number of tips:

round(fit$lik.anc[1, ], 3)
laur <- getMRCA(t2, c("Bos_taurus", "Sorex_araneus"))
round(fit$lik.anc[laur - Ntip(t2), ], 3)
laur_tips <- extract.clade(t2, laur)$tip.label
length(laur_tips)
table(x[laur_tips])
names(x[laur_tips])[x[laur_tips] == "S"]
table(x[!(names(x) %in% laur_tips)])
Check

The root of the mammals had S (probability 1.00); the ancestor of the Laurasiatheria, about 79 million years ago, had T (1.00). Of the 272 laurasiatherians, 268 have T; the four with S are three pipistrelle bats and the European mole. All 187 other mammals have S.

Show the two ancestors on the tree, as pies of their probabilities:

plot(t2, type = "fan", show.tip.label = FALSE, edge.width = 0.5)
tiplabels(pch = 19, col = cols[x], cex = 0.5)
nodelabels(node = Ntip(t2) + 1, pie = fit$lik.anc[1, , drop = FALSE], piecol = cols, cex = 1.2)
nodelabels(node = laur, pie = fit$lik.anc[laur - Ntip(t2), , drop = FALSE], piecol = cols, cex = 1.2)
legend("topleft", legend = c("S (serine)", "T (threonine)"), col = cols, pch = 19, bty = "n")

Save it for your report (as in every stage from now on: png(), the plotting code, dev.off()), as ~/project/figures/fig_grm1_tree.png.

Count the changes

Give each node its most likely amino acid, then compare the two ends of every branch: where they differ, the amino acid changed on that branch. t2$edge is a table of the branches, the node they come from in the first column and the node or tip they lead to in the second:

anc <- colnames(fit$lik.anc)[apply(fit$lik.anc, 1, which.max)]
states <- c(x, anc)          # tips first, then the nodes, in the tree's numbering
from <- states[t2$edge[, 1]]
to <- states[t2$edge[, 2]]
table(from, to)
Check

Three changes in 179 million years of mammal evolution: one S→T, on the branch to the Laurasiatheria, and two T→S, back again (in the pipistrelles and in the mole). All 268 T species inherited their T from one ancestor.

A pattern as different from GRM1's as it could be.

5. The porcupine

NPSR1 206 has H in a single species, the Brazilian porcupine. What do its relatives have? Look for the stretch around 206 – PD, any one amino acid, S, any one, WTPY – in the TOGA proteins of the porcupines and the guinea pig, in the terminal. awk's match finds a pattern in a string, and substr($2, RSTART, RLENGTH) prints what it found:

seqtk seq -l0 /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/NPSR1_toga_mammals.fasta | paste - - | grep -E "^>(Homo_sapiens|Coendou_prehensilis|Erethizon_dorsatum|Hystrix_cristata|Cavia_porcellus) " | awk -F'\t' '{split($1, n, " "); match($2, /PD.S.WTPY/); print substr(n[1], 2), substr($2, RSTART, RLENGTH)}'

Check

The human, the guinea pig and the North American porcupine Erethizon dorsatum have PDDSYWTPY; the Brazilian porcupine PDDSHWTPY; the crested porcupine PDNSCWTPY – C at 206, and another change two positions before it. The two American porcupines split about 10.5 million years ago.

The North American porcupine is not in your table of stage 4: its protein is fine, but kalign put another stretch of it against human 196–216, and the window check set it aside – a check that throws out a good sequence now and then is better than one that lets a bad one in. Its Y says that if the Brazilian porcupine's H is real, it arose on the Brazilian porcupine's own branch, in the last ten million years – one change, in one species. Is it real? That needs the porcupine's raw DNA (stage 10).
