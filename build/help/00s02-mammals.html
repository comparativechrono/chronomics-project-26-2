Stage 2 · Hundreds of mammals

Weeks 2–3about 5 hours

Every mammal has the five genes of the short-sleep mutations: the same gene in different species is called an ortholog. In this stage you meet their proteins, from two independent sources, and find out what the files hold – which species, how long the proteins are, and why the numbers of the human protein do not work for a mouse.

You will learn
  • where the sequences come from: NCBI's annotation of genomes, and TOGA
  • to list and count what is in a FASTA file, and to search it – and the traps of searching
  • to compare two lists with comm
  • a for loop over files, and sort by numbers
  • why different species need to be aligned before they can be compared

1. Two sources, eleven files

cd ~/project
ls /data/examples/fnss/orthologs
head -4 /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta

There are two files for each gene. The ncbi files come from NCBI, which annotates newly sequenced genomes – predicts their genes and proteins from RNA and from known proteins – in its RefSeq collection, and works out which genes of different species are orthologs. The toga files come from TOGA (Kirilenko et al. 2023), which aligns the genome of each species to the human genome and carries each human gene across, reading off the protein that the other genome would make. (GRM1b – mGluR1b – has only an NCBI file: TOGA predicts one form of each gene.)

Each name line starts with the species, written Genus_species, then the accession of the protein (NCBI) or the name of the genome assembly (TOGA), then the species' common name. The human protein is first in every file.

grep -c ">" /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/*.fasta

* stands for "anything": *.fasta is every file whose name ends in .fasta. With several files, grep -c gives a count for each.

Check

NCBI: BHLHE41 240 sequences, ADRB1 261, NPSR1 254, GRM1 256, GRM1b 203, SIK3 266. TOGA: BHLHE41 300, ADRB1 339, NPSR1 370, GRM1 394, SIK3 402. One sequence per species, the human one included.

2. Who is in them?

grep ">" /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta | head
grep -i "elephant" /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta
grep ">Mus_musculus " /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/*_ncbi_mammals.fasta

grep -i ignores the difference between capitals and small letters. Look at what "elephant" finds: an elephant, but also an elephant shrew and two elephant seals. grep finds letters, not meaning – always look at what it found before you count it.

Compare the two lists

Which species are in both sources, and which in one only? Make a sorted list of the species of each file: cut -d " " -f 1 keeps the first word of each name line (-d " ": the columns are separated by spaces), and cut -c 2- removes the >.

grep ">" /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta | cut -d " " -f 1 | cut -c 2- | sort > results/BHLHE41_ncbi_species.txt
grep ">" /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_toga_mammals.fasta | cut -d " " -f 1 | cut -c 2- | sort > results/BHLHE41_toga_species.txt
wc -l results/BHLHE41_*_species.txt

comm compares two sorted lists. It prints three columns – lines only in the first file, lines only in the second, lines in both – and -1, -2, -3 hide them:

comm -12 results/BHLHE41_ncbi_species.txt results/BHLHE41_toga_species.txt | wc -l
comm -23 results/BHLHE41_ncbi_species.txt results/BHLHE41_toga_species.txt | wc -l
comm -13 results/BHLHE41_ncbi_species.txt results/BHLHE41_toga_species.txt | wc -l
Check

135 species are in both, 105 only in NCBI's file and 165 only in TOGA's – 405 species in all, the human included.

Then look at the assemblies in TOGA's file: grep ">" …toga… | cut -d " " -f 2 | cut -c 1-2 | sort | uniq -c. Most names start with HL – the genomes assembled by the Hiller lab, which made TOGA, from the reads of many projects; the others are the names of the UCSC Genome Browser. Why might a species be in one source and not the other?

Show the answer

NCBI only annotates some genomes, and a species is in its file only if NCBI's annotation found a good DEC2 protein in it; TOGA's alignments with the human genes include only placental mammals – which is why all the marsupials of NCBI's file, from the koala to the Tasmanian devil, are missing from TOGA's – and TOGA kept only the predictions it was sure of. The two sources were built differently, from partly different genomes – which is what makes them useful checks on each other.

3. How long are the proteins?

seqtk comp writes a line for each sequence: its name, its length, and then counts of A, C, G and T, which mean nothing for a protein – keep the first two columns. sort -n sorts by number, and sort -k2,2n by the number in the second column:

seqtk comp /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta | head -3 | cut -f 1,2
seqtk comp /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta | cut -f 2 | sort -n | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -5
seqtk comp /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta | sort -k2,2n | head -3 | cut -f 1,2
seqtk comp /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta | sort -k2,2n | tail -3 | cut -f 1,2
Check

The commonest length is 482 – the human one – in 30 species, then 484 (21) and 483 (12). The shortest is the Alpine marmot's, 292; the longest, 597, belong to three hoofed mammals (a buffalo, the white-tailed deer and the bighorn sheep).

Now all eleven files at once, with a for loop: $f takes the name of each file in turn, and basename removes the folders from it:

for f in /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/*.fasta
do
  echo "$(basename $f) $(grep -c '>' $f) $(seqtk comp $f | cut -f 2 | sort -n | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -1)"
done

Each line: the file, its number of sequences, and its commonest length with how many species have it.

Think

Why do the proteins of one gene differ in length? Some differences are real – an insertion or deletion in the gene of one group of species. Some come from the annotation: a species may make several forms of a protein, and a prediction may miss an exon or add one. (Unadkat & Whittall 2020 found a gorilla DEC2 with 106 extra amino acids at one end and 65 missing at the other, and suggested it was an annotation error.) And some come from the genome itself: a gap in an assembly, or a misread letter that breaks the reading frame. TOGA writes X for a codon it could not read. Note too that TOGA's human NPSR1 is 390 amino acids, not 371: TOGA used another isoform, the same as yours up to position 340.

4. Position 384 is not 384

Turn the DEC2 file into a table, as in stage 1, and print, for seven species, the amino acid at position 384, the 21 around it, and the length of the protein. Here awk's substr($2, 374, 21) takes 21 letters of the sequence from position 374, and -v sp=$sp hands the species' name to awk:

seqtk seq -l0 /data/examples/fnss/orthologs/BHLHE41_ncbi_mammals.fasta | paste - - > results/BHLHE41_ncbi.tsv
for sp in Homo_sapiens Pan_troglodytes Mus_musculus Rattus_norvegicus Canis_lupus Bos_taurus Loxodonta_africana
do
  grep "^>$sp " results/BHLHE41_ncbi.tsv | awk -F'\t' -v sp=$sp '{print sp, substr($2, 384, 1), substr($2, 374, 21), length($2)}'
done

The chimpanzee has P at 384, in the same stretch as the human. But the mouse and the rat have H, in a stretch that looks nothing like it; the dog has Y – in the stretch around human Y362; the cow has A. Is DEC2's proline missing from these species? Search for the stretch itself instead of a position: grep -o prints only the part of the line that matched, and -b adds where in the line it starts, counting from 0:

grep "^>Homo_sapiens " results/BHLHE41_ncbi.tsv | cut -f 2 | grep -o -b "PFPLLYPG"
grep "^>Mus_musculus " results/BHLHE41_ncbi.tsv | cut -f 2 | grep -o -b "PFPLLYPG"
cut -f 2 results/BHLHE41_ncbi.tsv | grep -c "PFPLLYPG"
Check

The human PFPLLYPG starts 383 letters in – at position 384, which is P384. The mouse has the same stretch 324 letters in: the mouse's proline that matches human P384 is its P325. The stretch is found, letter for letter, in 229 of the 240 proteins.

Your turn

Which 11 species do not have PFPLLYPG? (grep -v keeps the lines that do not match.) What do most of them have in common?

Show the answer 
grep -v "PFPLLYPG" results/BHLHE41_ncbi.tsv | cut -f 1 | cut -d " " -f 1,3-

Ten of the eleven are marsupials – the koala, wallaby, Tasmanian devil, opossums and others – whose last common ancestor with us lived about 160 million years ago; their DEC2 has a slightly different stretch here. Searching for an exact stretch misses them: what is needed is a method that lines up similar, not only identical, sequences.

Numbers belong to one sequence. The mouse protein is shorter (410 amino acids) – it lacks a stretch before this point – so everything after the gap is numbered differently. To find "position 384" in 400 species you need to line the sequences up, letter against matching letter, allowing for gaps: a multiple sequence alignment. That is stage 3.
