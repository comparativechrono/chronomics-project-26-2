The science: sleep, short sleepers and evolution

Week 1about 45 minutes, then the reading list

What you need to know before you start: how the brain controls sleep, which mutations shorten it, what it means for an amino acid to be "conserved" – and why that claim, made in every paper on these mutations, has never been tested properly.

What decides how long we sleep

Two processes time our sleep. A circadian clock, whose master is the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, makes us sleepy at one time of day and alert at another (process C). A homeostat keeps account of how long we have been awake: the longer we are up, the greater the pressure to sleep, seen in the slow waves of the EEG in deep sleep; sleep pays the debt off (process S). Under both lies a switch between wake and sleep. Its backbone is made of neurons of the brainstem, hypothalamus and basal forebrain that use the fast transmitters glutamate and GABA: some keep the cortex awake, others switch them off to let us sleep. Other systems tune the switch – noradrenaline from the locus coeruleus, histamine, acetylcholine and the neuropeptide orexin (also called hypocretin), which helps to keep us awake once we are awake: people who lose their orexin neurons have narcolepsy (Saper & Fuller 2017).

How long people sleep varies, and part of the variation is inherited. Genome-wide association studies of hundreds of thousands of people find dozens of places in the genome where a common variant goes with longer or shorter sleep – 78 for sleep duration in UK Biobank (Dashti et al. 2019) – but each shifts sleep by a few minutes at most.

Natural short sleepers

A few people are different. All their lives they sleep four to six and a half hours a night, feel rested, and do not seem to need more sleep. In some families this familial natural short sleep (FNSS) is passed on from parent to child: one copy of a mutation is enough. In 2009 the group of Ying-Hui Fu found the first gene by sequencing clock genes in a small family: the two members who slept, by their own account, 6.25 hours a day, against 8.06 hours for the others, carried a mutation in DEC2 (also called BHLHE41), and mice given the mutant human gene slept less (He et al. 2009). Six more have been reported since. Five were found in the same way, mostly by the same group – sequence the exomes of a family, or of one person, who sleep little; find a rare change; make mice that carry it. The sixth, a second change in DEC2, came from sequencing the gene in the volunteers of two studies of sleep deprivation.

Two things are worth noticing. The evidence in people is thin: one or two families per mutation, sometimes one person, sleep mostly reported by the people themselves. And the mouse evidence is indirect: mice sleep in short bouts through the day and night, and a mouse that sleeps an hour less is not a human who sleeps two hours less. When carriers of four of them were looked for in large studies of the general population – UK Biobank and others – they did not sleep unusually little (Weedon et al. 2022). You will meet that again in stage 9.

Conserved amino acids

Proteins change as species evolve: a mutation that changes an amino acid now and then spreads through a species. But a change that damages a protein the body needs is removed again by natural selection – carriers leave fewer offspring – and so the amino acids that matter most stay the same over long stretches of evolution. They are conserved, and the force that keeps them is purifying selection. That is why a conserved amino acid is taken as a sign of an important one, and why a mutation there is more believable as the cause of a disease or a trait.

Nearly every paper on the short-sleep mutations makes this argument. He et al. (2009) wrote that the proline "is conserved in mammals but not invertebrates"; Shi et al. (2019), that the alanine of ADRB1 is "highly conserved in vertebrates and invertebrates"; Xing et al. (2019), that the tyrosine of NPSR1 lies in "one of the highly conserved extracellular domains"; Shi et al. (2021), that the serine of GRM1 is "conserved among vertebrates"; Chen et al. (2025), that the asparagine of SIK3 "is conserved among mammals and birds". Each claim rests on a figure of a handful of species. Today there are genomes of hundreds of mammals, and the claim can be measured rather than stated: in how many species is the amino acid the same, and is it more or less conserved than the rest of its protein? Where it has changed, when did that happen?

How long mammals sleep

Sleep has been measured in more than a hundred species of mammal, most often with the EEG, and they differ more than any group of people: from about three hours a day in horses and cows to twenty in some armadillos and opossums. Plant-eaters tend to sleep less than meat-eaters, and animals that sleep in exposed places less than those that sleep in burrows (Capellini et al. 2008). Large animals tend to sleep less than small ones (Siegel 2005) – but once Capellini and colleagues allowed for the family tree, body size no longer predicted sleep: a warning you will meet again in stage 7. Dolphins and whales sleep with one half of the brain at a time, the other half awake (Lyamin et al. 2008).

That variety makes a natural experiment. If one changed amino acid can shorten sleep in a family, perhaps evolution has used the same changes: mammals that sleep little might carry, at these positions, the amino acids of the short sleepers – or at least amino acids different from those of mammals that sleep a lot. But there is a catch. Species are not independent of each other: cows and sheep sleep little and share many amino acids because they had a common ancestor not long ago, not because one causes the other. Studies across species have to allow for the family tree (Felsenstein 1985) – you will, in stage 7.

Where the sequences come from

Hundreds of mammal genomes have now been sequenced and assembled. NCBI predicts the genes and proteins of many of them in its RefSeq annotation and lists which genes of different species correspond – their orthologs. TOGA (Kirilenko et al. 2023) takes another route: it aligns each genome to the human one and carries the human genes across, predicting each gene's protein in each species even where nobody has annotated the genome. You will use both, and compare them. Each makes mistakes; where two independent sources agree, you can trust the answer more.

The questions of this project

  1. Conservation. Are the seven amino acids the same in all mammals, as the papers say? How conserved are they compared with the rest of their proteins? (Stages 1–4)
  2. History. Where they differ, where on the mammal family tree did they change, and how many times? (Stage 5)
  3. Sleep. Do species with a different amino acid at these positions sleep a different amount? (Stages 6–7)
  4. Structure. Where do the amino acids sit in the proteins' three-dimensional shapes? (Stage 8)
  5. People. How common are the mutations, and do the people who carry them sleep less? (Stage 9)
  6. Is there a mammal with a short-sleep mutation of its own? (Stage 10)
