Stage 1 · Seven mutations

Weeks 1–2about 5 hours

Before you compare hundreds of species, get to know the seven mutations and the human proteins they change. In this stage you learn to give the computer instructions by typing them, and use them to find each mutated amino acid yourself – and to check that the papers' numbers are right.

You will learn
  • the terminal: commands, options and arguments; files, folders and paths
  • to look into files without opening them: head, cut, grep, wc, sort, uniq – and to join them with pipes, |
  • tables in text files, and proteins in FASTA files
  • a loop: the same commands for every line of a table
  • to look up a protein in UniProt and a paper in Europe PMC

1. Your first commands

The Terminal tab on the right is a Linux terminal, the way most bioinformatics is done. The line that ends in $ is the prompt: the computer is waiting for a command. Type one and press Enter. Type these four yourself – one at a time – rather than using the button:

pwd
ls /data/examples/fnss
ls -l /data/examples/fnss/orthologs
ls -lh /data/examples/fnss/orthologs
  • pwd – print working directory: the folder you are in. It is your home folder, /home/user, which can also be written ~.
  • ls – list the files in a folder. /data/examples/fnss is the folder of the project's data (you can read it but not change it).
  • -l is an option: it makes ls show a long list, with the size and date of each file. -lh shows the sizes in human units (K, M). Options change what a command does; arguments (here the folder) tell it what to work on.

Three things save a lot of typing: Tab completes the name of a file or a command (type ls /data/ex and press Tab); ↑ brings back the commands you typed before; and man ls (or ls --help) explains a command and all its options. help lists the programs of this terminal.

Your project folder

Keep everything you make in one place. Make a folder, project, with three folders in it – for results, figures and scripts – and go into it:

mkdir -p ~/project/results ~/project/figures ~/project/scripts
cd ~/project
ls

mkdir makes folders (-p: with any folders above them, and no complaint if they exist already); cd – change directory – moves you into one. A path says where a file is: /data/examples/fnss/human starts at the top of the file system (an absolute path); results/human_proteins.tsv starts from where you are (a relative path); ~ is your home; .. is the folder above.

2. A table of mutations

The seven mutations are listed in variants.tsv, a table kept as text: one line per row, with a tab between the columns (.tsv: tab-separated values). head shows the first lines of a file – here the line of column names and the first mutation:

head -2 /data/examples/fnss/variants.tsv

The lines are long and wrap. cut keeps only some columns (-f: fields): here the first six. And head -1 … | tr '\t' '\n' | cat -n turns the line of names into a numbered list, so you know which number each column has. The pipe, |, sends the output of one command into the next:

cut -f 1-6 /data/examples/fnss/variants.tsv
head -1 /data/examples/fnss/variants.tsv | tr '\t' '\n' | cat -n

Count the lines, and the genes – tail -n +2 starts at line 2 (leaving out the names), sort puts equal lines next to each other, and uniq -c counts them:

wc -l /data/examples/fnss/variants.tsv
cut -f 2 /data/examples/fnss/variants.tsv | tail -n +2 | sort | uniq -c
Check

9 lines: the names and 8 rows. Seven are the short-sleep mutations, in five genes – BHLHE41 has two (P384R and Y362H), and so does GRM1, one of them numbered in its isoform GRM1b. The eighth, NPSR1 N107I, is a common variant that the project uses for comparison (column 15, kind).

3. Proteins are text too

A protein sequence is written as a string of one-letter codes for its amino acids: M for methionine (the first amino acid of nearly every protein), P proline, R arginine, Y tyrosine, H histidine, A alanine, V valine, S serine, T threonine, N asparagine, W tryptophan… Sequences are kept in FASTA files: a line that starts with > and names the sequence, then the sequence itself, a few dozen letters to a line.

head -3 /data/examples/fnss/human/human_proteins.fasta
grep ">" /data/examples/fnss/human/human_proteins.fasta
grep -c ">" /data/examples/fnss/human/human_proteins.fasta

grep prints the lines that contain a pattern – here >, so the name lines – and grep -c counts them. Each name line has the name the project uses for the protein, its RefSeq accession and a description.

A sequence split over many lines is awkward to work with. seqtk seq -l0 writes each sequence on one line, and paste - - joins every two lines into one, with a tab between them: name, then sequence. That turns the FASTA file into a table, which you keep in results:

seqtk seq -l0 /data/examples/fnss/human/human_proteins.fasta | head -2 | cut -c 1-80
seqtk seq -l0 /data/examples/fnss/human/human_proteins.fasta | paste - - > results/human_proteins.tsv
cut -f 1 results/human_proteins.tsv

> at the end of a command (outside quotes) sends its output into a file instead of the screen – careful: it replaces whatever the file held. cut -c 1-80 cuts each line to its first 80 characters.

How long is each protein? awk is the tool for columns: -F'\t' says they are separated by tabs, $1 and $2 are the first and second, length($2) counts the letters of the second, and split and substr take the first word of the name without its >:

awk -F'\t' '{split($1, name, " "); print substr(name[1], 2), length($2)}' results/human_proteins.tsv
Check

Six proteins: BHLHE41 482 amino acids, ADRB1 477, NPSR1 371, GRM1 1194, GRM1b 906 and SIK3 1369.

4. Find the mutated amino acids

DEC2's mutation is P384R: proline at position 384 changed to arginine. Is there a P at position 384 of the human protein? grep "^>BHLHE41 " keeps the line of BHLHE41 (^ means "at the start of the line", and the space after the name stops GRM1 from matching GRM1b too); cut -f 2 keeps the sequence; cut -c 384 its 384th letter:

grep "^>BHLHE41 " results/human_proteins.tsv | cut -f 2 | cut -c 384
grep "^>BHLHE41 " results/human_proteins.tsv | cut -f 2 | cut -c 374-394
Check

Position 384 is P, and the 21 amino acids around it (374–394) are KYLYPAAAAAPFPLLYPGIPA: P384 is the eleventh, the first P of PFPLLYPG. A stretch rich in prolines and alanines – the "proline-rich region" of the paper.

"R889W" only makes sense in mGluR1b. A position always belongs to one particular protein sequence.

All eight at once: a loop

Doing this by hand for every mutation is slow and invites slips. A loop does it for each line of the table. while read variant gene pos aa reads one line at a time into four variables; inside the loop, $gene and $pos stand for their values, and $( … ) catches the output of a command into a variable:

tail -n +2 /data/examples/fnss/variants.tsv | cut -f 1,2,4,5 | while read variant gene pos aa
do
  found=$(grep "^>$gene " results/human_proteins.tsv | cut -f 2 | cut -c "$pos")
  echo "$variant: expected $aa, found $found"
done
Check

Eight lines, and in each the amino acid found is the one expected: BHLHE41_P384R: expected P, found P … NPSR1_N107I: expected N, found N.

Keep the 21 amino acids around each mutation for later – a table of two columns, written with printf (%s is replaced by a value, \t is a tab and \n the end of a line). $((pos - 10)) does arithmetic:

tail -n +2 /data/examples/fnss/variants.tsv | cut -f 1,2,4 | while read variant gene pos
do
  context=$(grep "^>$gene " results/human_proteins.tsv | cut -f 2 | cut -c $((pos - 10))-$((pos + 10)))
  printf "%s\t%s\n" "$variant" "$context"
done > results/mutation_context.tsv
cat results/mutation_context.tsv
Think

Look at the eight stretches. Two are rich in prolines (P), as is typical of parts of proteins without a fixed shape; one (ADRB1's) is full of amino acids that avoid water – A, I, L, V, F – as is typical of a stretch that crosses a cell membrane. You will see whether these guesses are right in stage 8.
